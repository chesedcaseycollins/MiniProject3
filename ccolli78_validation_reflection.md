# Validation Reflection — ccolli78 (Casey Collins)

Benjamin Noyes (bnoyes) and I validated each other's MiniProject2 results, 100 records in total (five fields across ten projects each). Before discussion we disagreed on 24 of them: 21 in my validation of Benjamin's projects and 3 in his validation of mine. The split was uneven for a simple reason. Benjamin used his own vocabulary for ActivityPattern ("frequent bursts", "occasional activity", "consistent bursts") rather than the codebook's seven labels, so all ten of those rows were flagged even though several, like "consistent bursts" for opengeospatial_teamengine, meant the same thing as the codebook label (Steady). Once we mapped his labels onto the codebook using commits per year, every disagreement resolved. The remaining disagreements were more substantive: three WhyRecovered reasons that were too generic, three Currentstatus calls that hinged on what "last commit" means, seven RecentThemes rows affected by snapshot drift or category choice, and one HasRecovered label ("Partially") that sat outside the yes/no vocabulary. After discussion we agreed on all 24. Pre-discussion agreement ranged from 50% (ActivityPattern) to 95% (HasRecovered), and Cohen's kappa was low for Currentstatus (0.35) despite 85% raw agreement because almost every project is Active, so chance agreement is high.

Looking across the adjudicated labels, the signals that precede a long gap were consistent. Projects that stopped had usually already thinned out to a single maintainer and a single-digit commit year before the gap: badlands-model_badlands had six commits in 2018 before its gap, helske_kfas dropped from 139 commits in 2015 to 15 in 2017, and jackwasey_icd was a one-person package before its 36-month silence. A release followed by silence was another recurring pattern. opengeospatial_teamengine shipped 6.0.0 in March 2025 and the default branch has not moved since, and metagenome-atlas_atlas updated its CHANGELOG and cut a release just before `main` went dormant. The pre-gap commit themes tended to be cleanup, formatting and dependency pinning rather than features, which reads in hindsight as a maintainer tidying up before stepping away.

Recovery triggers fell into three groups. The most common was ecosystem breakage: badlands came back as a Python 3 port, soft-matter_trackpy came back when NumPy and SciPy deprecations broke it, philipperemy_keras-tcn revived for TensorFlow 2.19, and the late burst in jackwasey_icd was entirely about passing modern R checks. The second was a maintainer clearing a backlog of contributor pull requests, as in libnegf_libnegf (two pending PRs merged on the same day) and oxfordcontrol_cosmo.jl (a crash fix from a second maintainer). The third was a release or institutional event: davidcarslaw_openair restarted for a CRAN release, qutech-delft_quantuminspire for its 1.2.0 release, and helske_kfas when its sole maintainer reappeared under a new university email. In almost every case the people driving recovery were the original maintainers, not newcomers, which makes the "sole maintainer" signal before a gap a double-edged one: the project is fragile, but it also tends to come back when that one person returns.

Gaps did not reliably mean death. devinamatthews_tblis sat in a trough from 2022 to 2024 and then had its busiest year in 2025, which is why it was adjudicated U-shaped rather than Declining. openair and quantuminspire are also U-shaped and now more active than before their gaps. teamengine has had exactly one three-month gap in nineteen years. On the other hand, a commit after a gap is not automatically recovery: scify_jedaitoolkit's only post-gap activity is a Dependabot bump and the repository is archived, and jackwasey_icd's gap ended with a lone README edit. We ended up applying an unwritten threshold, that recovery needs human commits with a purpose, and the codebook should say so.

The interpretation pitfalls we hit were mostly about the codebook rather than the projects. First, vocabulary: both of us drifted outside the allowed labels (Benjamin's activity descriptions, my "Partially" and "Yes (modestly)"), which inflates disagreement counts without reflecting real differences in judgement. Second, the Currentstatus rule does not say whether "last commit" means the default branch or any branch; atlas and teamengine look Active if unmerged bot and WIP branches count and Inactive if they do not. We settled on the default branch. Third, the reference date of 2025-09-30 is stale, and if the six-month window is measured from today two more projects flip to Inactive. Fourth, RecentThemes is sensitive to when the data was pulled; three of my seven RecentThemes disagreements were cases where Benjamin's answer was right for his snapshot and mine was right for GitHub today, so we agreed to grade against the author's snapshot. Fifth, the theme categories have no home for CI and build tooling, which made up six of the latest ten commits in helske_kfas, and Dependabot commits can be coded as either Dependency updates or Automated bot contributions. Each of these became a concrete codebook suggestion: fix the reference date, name the default branch, define a minimum for recovery, add a CI/tooling theme, state a convention for bot commits, and give a tie-break rule for the longest gap (libnegf has two seven-month gaps). With those changes most of our 24 disagreements would not have occurred.
